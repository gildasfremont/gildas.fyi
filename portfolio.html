Ornikar

Getting 15,000 people through 96 administrations

First product manager, employee nº 8 · 2014–2016 · Regulated B2C marketplace

Zero to 15,000 paying users in 18 months Strategic Design Award (2015) $145M raised 2018–2021
TL;DR

Growth looked like an acquisition problem and was a registration problem: ninety-six administrations, each with its own unwritten rules, and a population about whom nothing can be assumed. We did the registrations by hand to learn the rules, automated them once they were stable, then cut the friction on the user side. Zero to 15,000 paying users in eighteen months.

The situation

Learning to drive in France went through driving schools. Ornikar sold lessons without one, with neither a platform nor a legal framework for doing it online. I was the first product manager and built the team from there. From the outside the growth question looked like an acquisition question. It was not.

Where the product actually broke

A candidate registering for the test outside a school deals with the departmental service of their own département: ninety-six of them, each with its own forms, delays and reading of the rules. And the rules were written nowhere we could read them; they existed as practice in ninety-six offices.

The second constraint is the population: everyone who takes the test. Nothing can be assumed about the user, not their equipment, not their ease with written French, not whether their papers are valid. Every design that relied on such an assumption failed on the first hundred users.

The decision: prioritise by friction removed, not by acquisition

What we didThe credible alternative
BetSpend product effort on the registration path until a user who pays gets registered without our help.Spend on acquisition to fill the funnel while registration stayed a manual, support-heavy process.
WhyOn a regulated market, an acquired user who fails to get registered is a refund, a ticket and a bad review. Friction is the leading indicator; growth follows with a delay.Faster to show a growth curve, easier to explain to investors. Compounds the registration problem with every cohort, and costs a support team the marketplace was not supposed to need.

Three moves, in this order

First by hand: a support team built in house did the registrations manually, one département at a time, and the purpose was to learn the real rules by performing them. Then automate: once a département's rules were stable in practice, we encoded them; automating first would have encoded guesses. Then back to the user side, forms, documents, messages, cutting whatever the automated path no longer needed from the user.

The order is the point. Each phase produced the knowledge the next one needed, and none of them could have been skipped.

What it produced

  • Zero to 15,000 paying users in eighteen months. The platform has since passed a million registrations.
  • A registration pipeline that scaled with the cohorts instead of against them.
  • Strategic design award, 2015. 145 million dollars raised between 2018 and 2021.

Lucca: salary review

Twenty candidate views, three that survived

Fractional product director · since 2023 · New product on a suite of 13 modules and 7,800 customers

Twenty views to three Now the sales demo feature 200+ days at Lucca
TL;DR

A new tool on a mature suite: the salary review, the campaign where managers propose raises inside a budget and HR consolidates and arbitrates. Twenty candidate views came out of customer requests, each one justified by a real way of running the campaign. Three survived, the ones that cover the review as it is actually run, and that three-view version is the one sales demo today. On a mature product the outcome I aim for is not a feature that ships but a version that sells.

The setting

Lucca's thirteen modules share an employee record, a permission model and a validation chain. A design decision taken in one of them surfaces in all the others, so there is no such thing as a local fix. Creating a product on such a suite is a particular exercise: the new tool inherits the design system, the business objects and the permissions, and it still has to be learnable on its own, by a manager who opens it once a year.

What a salary review is

Once a year, sometimes twice, a company decides who gets a raise and how much. Managers propose for their team inside an envelope, HR and finance consolidate, check the proposals against the budget, the pay policy and equity, a validation chain closes the campaign and payroll applies the result. Every company runs it a little differently: by envelope or by hierarchy, by job grade or by site, with one round or several. The tool has to hold all of that and still read as one process.

Twenty views

By the time the design started, twenty candidate views had accumulated, each one answering a real request from a real customer. That is the difficulty: none of them was wrong. A view by manager, a view by envelope, a view for the arbitration meeting, a view for the person who checks the totals: each made sense for the customer who asked for it, and each one built alone would have been a reasonable feature.

Shipping all twenty would have produced a tool nobody could learn, with every future change multiplied by twenty. Picking by popularity would have produced the same tool with a longer delay, because popularity measures who asked loudest, not what the campaign needs.

The decision: cut against the campaign as it is run

What we didThe credible alternatives
BetKeep three views, the ones that cover the review as it is actually run, and let the rest be a filter, an export or a job for the modules that already exist.Ship all twenty behind configuration, or rank them by number of requests and ship the top of the list.
WhyA campaign has a small number of moments, propose, arbitrate, close, and a view per moment is what a manager can learn in one campaign and a salesperson can show in one demo.Configuration moves the decision to the customer, who has less context than we do. Ranking by requests rewards the loudest customer and produces the same sprawl, later.

What it produced

That three-view version is the one sales demo today. A design decision became the commercial argument, which is the outcome I aim for on mature products: not a feature that ships, a version that sells. A demo is the hardest test a view count can take. In a quarter of an hour the prospect has to understand the campaign, and twenty views would have failed that test before the first question.

Lucca: impersonation

One mechanism that constrains naming, navigation and permissions

Fractional product director · since 2023 · Design system and cross-cutting patterns

One view instead of two Written up as a pattern Full article
TL;DR

In HR software a person's view must routinely be opened by someone else: a manager who approves, an assistant entering an absent director's leave, an administrator diagnosing a setting. Most software answers with a separate admin screen, then maintains two representations of the same data that drift apart. Impersonation keeps one view: the manager picks an employee, the screen becomes that employee's, every action is logged as the manager's. The mechanism is dull; what it forces on naming, navigation and permissions is not. It had structured Lucca's first products and the knowledge had evaporated, so I wrote it up as a pattern.

Where the word comes from

To impersonate: to take on someone else's identity. In computing the word took a precise meaning in the 1990s, first at Microsoft with Windows NT. When a system process needed to run an operation with the rights of a given user, it impersonated that user: it borrowed their security context for a moment without becoming them. The mechanism served debugging, administration and permission tests. The idea underneath is a temporary, traceable delegation: you act in someone else's context, and the action stays attributed to whoever initiated it.

At Lucca the concept was carried over from system infrastructure to the user interface. When an administrator or a manager needs to act in an employee's view, enter their leave, check their balances, adjust a setting, they impersonate that employee. They see their screen, with their data, in their context, and every action they take is traced under their own identity. That transposition, from system mechanism to interface pattern, is what makes it interesting for software design.

The problem it solves

In software deployed to a company's employees there is a category of features that could be called structural. They are not features in the usual sense, with a clean functional scope and a ticket. They are cross-cutting mechanisms that, once in place, constrain the shape of everything that comes after: navigation, the naming of views, the permission model, the traceability of actions. Impersonation is one of them.

An HR product presents each employee with a view of their own: their leave, their expense reports, their reviews. Their view, their data. Except that in the reality of a company, that personal view must regularly be consulted or modified by someone else: a manager who approves, an assistant who enters leave for an absent executive, an administrator diagnosing a configuration issue.

The naive answer is a separate admin view. That is what most software does, and that is where the trouble starts. A separate admin view duplicates part of the employee view, with its own display rules, its own forms, its own edge cases. You end up maintaining two representations of the same data model, and they diverge mechanically over time.

The mechanism

In the interface it takes the form of an employee selector built into the main view. When a manager selects a member of their team, the interface reformats to show what that employee sees, with their data and their context. Actions performed in that mode are attributed to the impersonator, not the impersonated, which preserves the audit trail. There is only one view to maintain. A dropdown and a context switch: technically, nothing.

Impersonation in the main viewA separate view per role
CostEvery view must survive being looked at by someone else: neutral names, navigation by workflow, author and owner modelled apart.Cheap on day one. One more screen, one more menu entry.
What you getOne representation of each object. The manager sees exactly what the employee sees, so support, training and bug reports talk about the same screen.Two representations that drift: display rules, forms and edge cases fixed twice or once, and nobody knows which.

The creative constraint

What makes impersonation interesting is not the mechanism but the set of constraints it imposes on the rest of the software, which end up improving its coherence. Take the naming of views. If an employee's main view can be opened by someone else, it can no longer be called "My leave" or "My schedule": the possessive stops working the moment a manager is looking at it on behalf of a team member. You are forced to find neutral names that describe the resource or the operation rather than the ownership relation, "Requests", "Schedule", "Account", and the nomenclature comes out clearer for everyone, the primary user included.

Same logic for navigation. If impersonation exists, the menu has to reflect the workflow of the software rather than the roles of its users. You cannot organise it as "employee view, manager view, admin view", because impersonation is precisely what erases that boundary. The menu represents the steps of the business process, and permissions decide what is visible, not the structure of the menu.

And the distinction between the author of an action and the owner of a record becomes explicit. When an assistant enters leave for an executive, the absence belongs to the executive and the entry is attributed to the assistant. Obvious once stated, and rarely modelled properly in software that lacks impersonation.

A pattern that fades

The paradox of this kind of pattern is that it is hard to transmit. It cannot be taught through a design system component and it does not fit in a functional spec. It is an architectural principle that product managers have to hold at design time, well before a designer opens Figma. If nobody carries it, it dilutes.

That is what I found at Lucca: impersonation had structured the first generation of products, and the knowledge had gone. Teams used the word without its architectural consequences. So I wrote it up in the form of a pattern, context, problem, forces, solution, consequences, in the sense of Christopher Alexander. Not to freeze a mechanism, but to make the forces at play explicit, so that design decisions stay coherent as teams grow and institutional memory dissolves.

Beyond Lucca

Impersonation is not specific to HR software. Any B2B or B2E product in which several role levels work on the same data faces the same question: duplicate the views by role, or build a context-switching mechanism? The answer depends on the complexity of the permission model and on the number of views involved, but the natural slope, duplicating, is almost always the wrong one.

The more general point is that every software product contains design choices that do not look like features and that determine the shape of every feature to come. Identifying them, naming them, formalising them as patterns is probably the most cost-effective design work one can do on a mature product. It is also the work that gets done the least, because it produces no visible deliverable and requires understanding the software as a whole rather than feature by feature. The full text is in Impersonation: anatomy of a pattern.

Lucca: admin roles

A permission model that leaked into the admin experience

Product design, framing, functional modelling · 2025, target usage 2026 · With Product, Customer Success and the roles task force

Data model mostly kept One primary role for the normal case Friction proportional to risk
TL;DR

Lucca's permission model could represent almost any HR access rule, and that flexibility leaked into the admin experience: an administrator had to rebuild the model in their head to configure it. Rather than rebuild the engine, we changed the mental model and the paths through it: one primary role for an employee's normal function, complementary accesses for the exceptions, and friction proportional to risk on sensitive permissions. Common configurations become simple, dangerous ones deliberate, effective access auditable. Most of the data model stays.

The model

In HR software, access to a piece of information is not a boolean. The same operation can be allowed on yourself, on the people you supervise, on a department, on several legal entities or on the whole company. What a user can do, combined with whom they can do it on, is the effective permission. The objects are few, and the trouble came from how they were exposed rather than from their number.

ObjectWhat it isExample
OperationAn elementary action or access defined by an application.Read a record, enter a sick leave, configure an export.
ScopeThe population the operation is allowed on.Self, supervised team, department, legal entities.
PermissionAn operation on one or more scopes.Read the individual situation of every employee.
Primary roleA user's normal function in the organisation.User, manager, administrator.
Complementary accessExtra permissions for a particular need.A temporary replacement, a works council member, testing a new application.
EntitlementThe assignment of a role or an access to a user.Alice gets the Manager role and an Expenses access.

Where it broke

Every user had exactly one primary role and any number of secondary roles. The two objects were close functionally and presented the same way, so a technical distinction produced no conceptual one: the administrator had to know when to enrich a primary role, when to create a secondary one, and keep the two coherent. Each new application made it worse. Its permissions were supposed to go into the existing roles when they belonged to people's normal function, and that was tedious enough that single-application secondary roles got created instead, one per user per app, when the doctrine was to have as few objects as possible.

Sensitive permissions were not visible enough: a label described an action, not everything it opened access to, so a permission could look right for a business need and expose personal data. Hierarchical, organisational and legal scopes followed different logics and sat in the same screens, so a correct interface produced wrong readings. And the most frequent operational question, why does this person see this data, cost more to answer than the configuration had cost to make, because the answer had to be rebuilt from several roles and the logs could not be read by user or by role.

One qualitative signal from Customer Success framed the target: in roughly nine cases out of ten they observed, a user would have needed a single multi-application role. Not an instrumented measure, but enough to design for a one-role happy path.

Reframing the problem

The first reading would have been "simplify the roles screen". A cleaner screen on the same mental model reduces visual load and leaves the structural risk intact. The question became: how do you make the common configuration close to trivial, keep the flexibility that enterprise cases need, and add guardrails proportional to risk, without a rewrite of the authorisation engine?

The decision: change the mental model, keep the engine

What we didThe credible alternative
BetKeep the technical objects, clarify what each one is for, add one structuring attribute, criticality, and rebuild the paths around the majority case.A greenfield redesign: a new taxonomy, distinct security groups, policies, a more explicit attribute-based engine.
WhyThe historical model carried real business value. The best simplification was an asymmetry of use and guardrails, not fewer objects.Cleaner on paper. Moves the problem to migration, compatibility and transformation cost, on thousands of live instances.

What changed

The primary role now means one thing: a user's function in the organisation. Permissions do not define the role, the function defines which permissions are needed, and that inversion is what makes a configuration doctrine stable enough to share between Product, Customer Success and customers. All managers share one Manager role; if a handful of them need expense management, nobody creates "Manager with expenses" and "Manager without", they keep Manager and get a complementary access.

Secondary roles are renamed complementary accesses, because "secondary" described a position in the data model and "complementary" encodes a rule: this object completes a primary role and corresponds to a particular situation, temporary, exceptional or reserved to a small population. The point is the asymmetry. Instead of two near-equivalent objects, a normal base and an exception layer.

Permissions carry a criticality, because reading ordinary team data, reading sensitive personal data, processing business data and changing the instance's configuration do not carry the same risk, and the interface should change behaviour with it rather than hope the administrator remembers.

LevelWhat it coversBehaviour
DefaultOrdinary permissions needed for everyday use.Added in one step.
SupervisionNon-sensitive data about one's team.Added in one step.
SensitivePersonal or sensitive data.Friction in a non-critical role.
ManagementBusiness data with real consequences.Critical roles only.
System configurationActions that change how the instance works.Friction even in a critical role.

Friction is not treated as a universal defect. On a reversible, low-risk action it slows people down for nothing; on a permission that can expose personal data or grant a destructive right, it is the prevention mechanism. So a role is declared critical or not before anyone is entitled to it, and that property bounds what can be added to it, how mass actions behave and whether it can be changed later. Management permissions cannot go into a non-critical role. Adding users in bulk to a critical role is blocked or limited. And the guardrails follow every path: a CSV import must run the same validations as the equivalent action in the interface, otherwise the screen is safe and the system is not.

The lifecycle, not the screen

Installing a new application is where the tensions concentrate: the people who configure and test it need access now, and the company's roles should not carry that access forever. Temporary complementary accesses cover installation and tests, the customer validates permissions and populations, and at go-live whatever belongs to normal use is merged into the primary roles and the temporary accesses are cleaned up, so that deploying a product on a mid-market instance no longer requires a complex intervention in role administration.

Investigation is turned around. An entitlements view starts from a person and goes up to the sources of their rights, rather than starting from each role to rebuild one person's situation, and the logs become a product component, readable by user, role, entitlement, permission and change. A diagnostic layer flags users with no access, large populations exposed to sensitive permissions and near-redundant roles, and proposes merges, with one safeguard: if two roles do not cover the same population, merging their permissions raises the rights of some users, so a structural clean-up cannot be treated as deduplication.

Migration does not pretend to know the criticality of every historical configuration. Roles with only non-critical permissions, and critical roles with a small population, can be classified; the ambiguous ones stay at "criticality unknown" and are shown as such. The system's uncertainty is exposed rather than replaced by an arbitrary label.

Alternatives set aside

AlternativeWhy not
Drop the primary / secondary distinctionSimpler vocabulary, but it removes the one useful piece of information, normal function versus exception, and moves the complexity to whatever object replaces it.
Single-application roles everywhereComposable for the engine, not for an administrator who wants to assign a person's normal status in one action.
A few multi-application roles for everyoneFewer objects, until each exception creates a variant and the combinations grow with every particular need.
Explanations and tooltips onlyDocumentation improves understanding. It prevents neither incoherent paths nor mistakes on sensitive permissions; an invariant of interaction does.

What this is, and is not

The visible deliverable, admin screens, add flows, an entitlements view, is the last layer. The work sits in four that condition each other: the modelling, role, access, permission, scope, entitlement, criticality; the doctrine, what the normal path is and what stays exceptional; security by interaction, prohibitions, limits and confirmations chosen by risk level; and the lifecycle, installation, tests, go-live, maintenance, audit and migration handled by one system.

It is a 2025 vision and doctrine with a 2026 target, not a shipped and measured release, and this page does not claim an uplift. The measures that would verify the direction are known: the share of users with only a primary role, the number of complementary accesses per user, the time and errors to set up a newcomer's access, the temporary accesses still present some days after go-live, the time to answer "why does this user have this right". Two points were still open at the time: the discovery of the diagnostic module, and the exact treatment of roles whose criticality cannot be inferred.

UBAQ

An audit that found the organisation behind the interface

Product audit, then ongoing support · NTU platform, healthcare compliance

Five interviews, one walkthrough Navigation rewrite started
TL;DR

A product team that could feel the interface working against them and had watched fixes fail to hold. Five interviews and a walkthrough, no user testing. The interface problems were symptoms: the cause was a company with no product expertise, a roadmap driven by migrations, and quick fixes that fed the next ones. Recommendations on three registers, interface, skills and process, so that repainting is not the only outcome.

The brief

UBAQ sells compliance software to the healthcare sector. The product team could feel the interface working against them, could not say where, and had watched several fixes fail to hold. There was no UX profile in the company and the team lived in permanent firefighting.

How I chose to look, and what I chose not to

UsedSet aside, on purpose
EvidenceFive internal interviews and a direct walkthrough of the application.End-user testing, analytics, heatmaps.
WhyInterviews cross what people believe the tool does with the constraints its builders were under: that is where the blind spots sit.On a niche B2B product the quantitative signal is mostly noise, and testing would confirm symptoms the team could already see.
ScopeThe gap between how the product behaves and what its users expect.Business decisions and functional coverage: not what to build, only why what exists misbehaves.

Level one: what the interface showed

The clearest example is an object the system calls a "manifestation". For the model it is one thing; for the sales users it is three, events, donations and contracts, and underneath those they think in spend and clients. One object bundling three concepts the users keep separate: Norman's gulf of evaluation, in a form you can point at.

The rest of the list is of the same kind. A kanban with no drag and drop and non-linear stages, a table in disguise promising a flow it does not show. A menu whose entries changed with the state of business objects. Forms holding four kinds of information with no hierarchy, two business flows on one screen: density from refusing to choose.

Level two: why they persisted

Every one of those could have been fixed on its own; the useful question was why they had not been. Client requests became features without a value question. The roadmap was driven by migrating customers off the previous product, one churn risk at a time. With no UX or product expertise in the room, features landed where they were cheap to build. Each quick fix added complexity, which raised the pressure, which produced quicker fixes.

Three registers, chosen by depth

RegisterWhat it changesWhy not alone
InterfaceObjects that match business concepts. A view built for the real flow instead of the kanban. Stable navigation. Ungrouped forms.Repainting without consolidating: the same causes rebuild the same interface.
SkillsUX and product expertise, hired, borrowed or grown.Better diagnosis of problems nobody has time to treat.
ProcessA prioritisation framework, a way out of reactive mode, protected time for coherence.A framework nobody can apply is a document.

What happened

The navigation rewrite started and the engagement continued with the product team. The useful part was not the interface list but the organisational diagnosis: it gave the team words for what was blocking them, and they began arbitrating differently. Five interviews are enough if run as an investigation, not as a wish list. The full reasoning is in Inside a product audit.

Guinet-Derriaz 1912

An information system for a group that had none

Information system from scratch · one year at two days a week · €100k budget

14 quarries, one shared catalogue
TL;DR

Fourteen quarries, ten million euros in revenue and no information system, on a hundred thousand euros and two days a week. I started with the product catalogue rather than the tools people asked for, because every tool would otherwise have sat on four incompatible versions of it. Teams that worked in silos now work off the same data.

Starting point

An industrial group in marble: fourteen quarries, around ten million euros in revenue, customers abroad, and no information system. Each team kept its own files and its own version of the catalogue; sales quoted from one, operations worked from another, and no question crossing two teams had a single answer.

The sequencing decision: catalogue first

With that budget and two days a week, the order of work is the strategy. The temptation is to start with what people ask for: tools for sales, tools for the quarries. I started with the catalogue, because every tool would have sat on four incompatible versions of it, and the first disagreement between two tools would have sent everyone back to spreadsheets.

So the shared database, built on one formalised catalogue, came first, before anyone saw a tool: the least visible piece and the one everything depends on. The tools for sales and for operations came on top of it.

Result

Teams that had worked in silos now work off the same data. At that budget, most of the work is deciding what not to build.

Luko by Allianz Direct

An unsolicited redesign, with the screens

Side project · March 2026 · Insurance customer area · Built with Claude in a few hours

Prototype live Claim: five steps from the home page Certificate: five interactions to three
TL;DR

A redesign of an insurance customer area done on my own account, so the only case here with screens. A policyholder logs in to get a document or to report a claim, and everything that did not serve those two was removed. Two flows rebuilt end to end: a claim reported from the home page in five steps, and a certificate in three interactions instead of five. Prototype live, built with Claude in a few hours.

Why this one is here

Unsolicited redesigns were a Dribbble fashion ten years ago and were rightly criticised: designing on a napkin without the product's real constraints is too easy. I include this one anyway, for two reasons. It is the only work on this page whose screens I own. And it shows what I do when nobody is paying me to do it, which is a fair signal of how I think.

I have been a Luko customer for years. I needed a liability certificate, and getting it meant opening the contract, finding the right accordion, understanding the certificate types, then generating the document. I talked it through with a former member of the Luko team, who confirmed the diagnosis: after the acquisition the team had changed a lot and product decisions had become hard to get built.

The audit, in three findings

Positioning. The customer area could not decide whether it was a management tool or an acquisition channel, and a product that serves two contradictory jobs fails at both. Trust. The chat switched to English when it crashed, near-empty pages loaded slowly, and a refresh could return an unrecovered 503 with the page in raw text, cookie banner included. Perceived performance is read as reliability. Hierarchy. Documents lived in three places, the home page blocks read like marketing pages, the newsletter prompt was more visible than the contracts, and an Allianz Travel banner followed you into the certificate form. The user's model, "I manage my insurance", did not match the architecture, "here are our offers".

The rule that drove the prototype

A policyholder logs in for one of two reasons: get a document, or report a claim. Everything that does not serve those two was removed. Three iterations followed, each driven by one principle. Progressive disclosure: the claim starts on the home page with the choice of property, actions appear in the context of each contract. Wording as UI: "add a contract" becomes "get insured", a system action replaced by the user's intent, and the real catalogue replaces a generic button that promised nothing. Proximity: the Documents tab disappears, documents belong to their contract, the profile sits under the avatar, and navigation goes from three tabs to none.

Reporting a claim: from the contract to the problem

Today, reporting a claim means finding the right contract first, then a link buried in its sub-menus, which presumes the user thinks in contract numbers rather than in the problem they have. The redesign starts from the problem, on the home page, and a five-step wizard (property, type, details, photos, confirmation) never sends the user back to a contract.

Getting a certificate: five interactions to three

From the home page the user clicks, picks the contract, and sees the four certificate types at once (liability, school, venue hire, holiday rental) with their use case as a subtitle. One click downloads the PDF. The current path takes five interactions, a scroll and an accordion.

The contract view: co-location

Each contract gets its own page: address, status, dates and yearly price at the top, then the claim for that property, then the certificates for that contract, then its documents with their dates, then guarantees, insured persons and payment as accordions. The original scatters these across the home page, a Documents tab and the contract sub-pages. The redesign puts each piece of information in the context that gives it meaning.

The prototype is live at dist-neon-delta-32.vercel.app and the full case is in Case study: Luko redesign with Claude. Audit, iterations and screens were produced with Claude in one short session: the reasoning is the work, the tool makes it cheap to show.
